# Persona and Awareness

Read this before writing anything where the reader is not already precisely defined, or where
the brief describes a "target audience" instead of a person. Copy written to a segment reads
like copy written to nobody.

---

## 1. The five awareness stages

Eugene Schwartz's model. The stage decides what the opening line must do, which is the single
highest-leverage decision in any piece of copy. Everything else follows from it.

### Unaware
The reader does not feel the problem, or does not connect it to anything solvable. They will
not read a headline about your product or even about the problem, because neither is on their
mind.

- **The opening must:** create self-recognition. A story, a scene, or a surprising fact that
  makes them think "that is me" before they know where it leads.
- **Do not:** name the product, name the category, or ask for anything.
- **Example opening:** "You wake up at 3am with the same thought you had at 3am yesterday."

### Problem-aware
The reader feels the pain and can describe it, but does not know that solutions exist, or
believes none work.

- **The opening must:** name the pain in their exact words, more precisely than they could
  themselves. Precision here is what earns the right to be read.
- **Then:** agitate. What does this cost them each week? What happens in a year if nothing
  changes?
- **Do not:** lead with features. They cannot evaluate a feature against a problem they have
  not yet framed as solvable.
- **Example opening:** "Every Monday you rebuild the same spreadsheet from last week's exports."

### Solution-aware
The reader knows solutions exist and may have tried some. They do not know yours, or do not
know why it would be different.

- **The opening must:** differentiate the mechanism. Not "we solve X" but "here is why X
  usually fails, and the different way this works."
- **Then:** proof that the mechanism holds.
- **Do not:** repeat the category promise every competitor makes. They have heard it.
- **Example opening:** "Most meal plans fail on Thursday. This one is built around Thursday."

### Product-aware
The reader knows your product and has not bought. Something is stopping them. That something
is the entire job of the copy.

- **The opening must:** address the objection directly, or stack proof against it.
- **Then:** reduce risk. Guarantee, trial, cancellation terms, what happens after they click.
- **Do not:** re-explain what the product is. They know.
- **Example opening:** "If you have wondered whether it works for a team of two, here is what
  a team of two does with it."

### Most-aware
The reader wants it and needs the offer. Terms, price, deadline if there is a real one, and the
CTA.

- **The opening must:** state the offer. Nothing else.
- **Do not:** add story or education. It delays the action they were ready to take.
- **Example opening:** "Annual plan, two months free, cancel any time before day 30."

### Diagnosing the stage from the brief
- Traffic from a search on the product name: product-aware or most-aware.
- Traffic from a search on the problem: problem-aware or solution-aware.
- Cold social ads to a lookalike audience: unaware or problem-aware.
- Retargeting after a site visit: product-aware.
- An email to an existing list: depends on what the list has been told. Ask.

When the brief mixes stages, write to the stage where the most valuable readers are, and let
the structure carry earlier-stage readers past the parts they do not need.

## 2. Market sophistication

Separate from awareness. Sophistication is how many similar claims the reader has already
heard from competitors. It decides how much claim the copy can make before it triggers
disbelief.

| Level | Market state | What works |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | First product of its kind | A simple, direct claim. "Lose weight." |
| 2 | Competitors have copied the claim | A bigger or more specific claim. "Lose 10 pounds in 30 days." |
| 3 | Claims have stopped working | A mechanism. "Lose weight by resetting the hormone that stores fat." |
| 4 | Mechanisms are everywhere | A refined or easier mechanism. "The 5-minute version of the reset." |
| 5 | Nothing is believed | Identification. Copy about who the reader is, not what the product does. |

Most digital categories are at level 3 or 4. If the copy is making a bare claim in a level 4
market, it is invisible. Move to mechanism or identification.

## 3. The persona sheet

Build this before drafting. Ten lines, filled with specifics. If a line would be true of
almost anyone, it is not filled in yet.

1. **Name and role.** One person. "Priya, operations lead at a 40-person logistics firm."
2. **Situation.** What is happening in their week that makes this relevant now.
3. **Pain, in their words.** A sentence they would actually say, not a marketing paraphrase.
4. **Dream Outcome.** The life change, not the feature. What Tuesday looks like after.
5. **What they have tried.** And why it did not stick.
6. **The one objection.** What they say to themselves that stops the click.
7. **What they need to believe** before they act. The one belief the copy must install.
8. **Who they trust.** Peers, a publication, a certification, a named authority.
9. **Vocabulary.** Words they use. Words they would find fake.
10. **Awareness stage and sophistication level.** From the sections above.

## 4. Mining the reader's own language

The best headline is almost always a sentence a customer already said. Look for it before
writing one.

**Where to look**
- Reviews of the product, and of competitors. One-star reviews reveal the objection. Five-star
  reviews reveal the Dream Outcome in the customer's words.
- Support tickets and sales call notes. The question asked most often is the objection.
- Forums and communities where the reader complains. Read how they describe the problem when no
  one is selling to them.
- Survey free-text answers. Skip the multiple choice; the free text is where the language is.

**What to extract**
- Verbatim pain phrases. Keep the exact wording, including the awkwardness.
- Verbatim outcome phrases. "I finally stopped checking my phone at dinner."
- Comparison language. "It is like X but for Y" tells you the mental category.
- Objection phrases. "I was worried that..." followed by what happened.

**How to use it**
- Lift verbatim phrases into headlines and openings. Polish lightly; do not rewrite into
  marketing voice.
- Use the reader's category word, not the brand's. If customers say "the schedule thing", the
  headline does not say "workforce orchestration platform".
- If no customer language is available, say so in the assumptions and write plainly. Never
  invent quotes to stand in for it.

## 5. The objection map

Every objection belongs to one of five types. Naming the type tells you what copy answers it
and where the answer goes.

| Type | Sounds like | Copy that answers it | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price | "Too expensive." "Not worth it." | Anchor against the cost of the problem. Reframe to per-day or per-use. Show the outcome value. | Near the price. Never before the value is established. |
| Time | "I do not have time to set this up." | Effort quantifier. "Set up in 4 minutes." Show the first step is small. | Under the CTA. |
| Trust | "Is this legit?" "Will they charge me?" | Transparency timeline. Named proof. Cancellation terms stated plainly. | Beside the CTA and near any payment step. |
| Fit | "Will it work for someone like me?" | Proof from someone like them. Specific use case named. | In the proof section, matched to the persona. |
| Effort | "This looks hard." "I will need to change everything." | "Without [pain]" framing. Show what stays the same. | In the subheadline and the how-it-works section. |

The intake step identifies one objection. Answer that one loudly. Answer the others quietly,
in the FAQ or in a subtext line. Copy that tries to answer all five with equal weight answers
none.
