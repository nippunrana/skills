# Persuasion Psychology for Copy

Read this when choosing which lever a piece of copy relies on, or when a draft feels flat and
you need to diagnose why. Each lever is described by its mechanism, when it works, when it
backfires, and how to apply it honestly. The honesty rules in SKILL.md apply to all of them.

The root principle: **the reader is asking "is this for me, and can I trust it?" before they
ask anything else.** Every lever below either answers one of those two questions or fails.

---

## 1. Attention levers

The first line has one job: earn the second line. The second line's job is to earn the third.
Copy is a slide, and any line the reader can stop at is a line that lost them.

### Specificity
**Mechanism.** A vague word ("fast", "easy", "affordable") forces the reader to supply a value,
and a skeptical reader supplies a bad one. A number or a concrete detail removes the gap.
Specific claims are also more believable, because they sound like something someone measured.
**Works when.** Always. This is the lever with no downside.
**Backfires when.** The specific number is invented. A fabricated statistic is worse than a
vague adjective, because it can be checked.
**Apply.** "Arrives in 23 minutes" over "fast delivery". "Cancel in one click before day 14"
over "easy cancellation". Where no number exists, use a sensory or situational detail
instead: "steps from the sand" over "close to the beach".

### Self-recognition
**Mechanism.** People attend to information about themselves before anything else. A line that
names the reader's situation is read; a line about the product is skimmed.
**Works when.** The persona is precise enough that the description is unmistakable.
**Backfires when.** The description is generic ("busy professionals") and the reader does not
feel singled out, or when it is wrong and the reader feels misread.
**Apply.** Open with the reader's situation: "You rebuild the same spreadsheet every Monday."
Use "you" in the first sentence.

### Curiosity gap
**Mechanism.** An open question the reader wants closed creates tension that only reading
resolves.
**Works when.** The gap is relevant to the offer and the copy closes it.
**Backfires when.** The gap is a trick ("You won't believe...") or the payoff is weak. Each
unrewarded click lowers the reader's trust for the next one.
**Apply.** "Most meal plans fail on Thursday" opens a gap (why Thursday?). The next line
closes it. Never open a loop the copy does not close.

### Pattern interrupt
**Mechanism.** Readers process familiar formats on autopilot. A break in the expected pattern
forces conscious attention.
**Works when.** Used once, at the opening.
**Backfires when.** Overused. A piece that is all interrupts has no pattern to interrupt.
**Apply.** A single short line where a paragraph is expected. A question where a claim is
expected. Plain language where jargon is expected.

### Cognitive ease
**Mechanism.** Copy that is easy to process is judged as more true and more likeable. Familiar
words, short sentences, and a predictable structure lower the effort of reading, and readers
credit that ease to the product.
**Works when.** Always, for every reader including experts.
**Backfires when.** Ease is confused with vagueness. Short and specific, not short and empty.
**Apply.** Reading age 12 to 14. One idea per sentence. Verbs over nouns: "we help you plan"
over "we provide planning assistance".

## 2. Decision levers

### Loss aversion
**Mechanism.** Losses are felt roughly twice as strongly as equivalent gains. "Stop losing 3
hours a week" moves more than "save 3 hours a week", for the same fact.
**Works when.** The loss is real, current, and the reader can feel it.
**Backfires when.** The loss is manufactured or exaggerated. Readers detect false urgency and
punish it with distrust.
**Apply.** Frame the cost of inaction, not just the benefit of action. Pair with specificity:
name the actual cost.

### Social proof
**Mechanism.** In uncertainty, people copy people like them. Proof works in proportion to how
similar the proof-giver is to the reader and how specific the result is.
**Works when.** Named, specific, and matched to the persona. "4,200 dental practices" for a
dental reader.
**Backfires when.** Vague ("trusted by thousands"), unmatched (enterprise logos for a solo
reader), or hidden in a carousel.
**Apply.** Lead with the number and the outcome. Place proof beside the decision it supports:
customer count near the hero, result-testimonials near pricing, ratings near the buy button.

### Authority
**Mechanism.** Credentials shortcut evaluation. A certification, a publication, a named
expert, or a regulatory approval lets the reader skip their own due diligence.
**Works when.** The authority is one the reader already respects.
**Backfires when.** The authority is irrelevant to the persona, or the copy leans on it instead
of on the outcome.
**Apply.** One line, near the claim it supports. Never as the headline.

### Scarcity and urgency
**Mechanism.** Limited availability raises perceived value and forces a decision now instead
of later. "Later" is where most conversions die.
**Works when.** The limit is real: a cohort with a fixed size, a launch price with a real end
date, a seasonal product.
**Backfires when.** Fake. Countdown timers that reset, "only 3 left" on a digital product,
"offer ends today" every day. This is the lever most often abused and the one readers are most
alert to.
**Apply.** State the real constraint and the real reason for it. "The March cohort is 20
seats because that is how many one coach can review." If there is no real constraint, use a
different lever.

### Anchoring
**Mechanism.** The first number seen sets the frame for every number after it. A price shown
after a higher reference reads as cheaper.
**Works when.** The anchor is relevant: the cost of the problem, the price of the alternative,
the value of the outcome.
**Backfires when.** The anchor is arbitrary or the crossed-out "was" price never existed.
**Apply.** Establish the cost of the problem before showing the price. Show the annual price
per month. State an add-on as a percentage of the main purchase.

### Reciprocity
**Mechanism.** Receiving something creates a pull to give back. Value delivered before the
ask makes the ask feel fair.
**Works when.** The gift is genuinely useful on its own.
**Backfires when.** The gift is a thin wrapper around a pitch, or the ask arrives before the
value is felt.
**Apply.** Give the tool, the checklist, the answer, or the first result. Then ask.

### Commitment and consistency
**Mechanism.** People act in line with what they have already done. A small yes makes the
next yes easier, because saying no would contradict the first.
**Works when.** The small step is real and the reader chose it.
**Backfires when.** The small step was a trick (a hidden opt-in) and the reader feels
trapped.
**Apply.** Sequence CTAs from light to heavy. "See how it works" before "Start my trial".
Let the reader invest something (a preference, a goal, a name) before asking for payment.

### Contrast
**Mechanism.** Value is judged relative to a nearby comparison, not in isolation. A number
next to a bigger number looks small.
**Works when.** The comparison is one the reader accepts as relevant.
**Backfires when.** The comparison is contrived.
**Apply.** State a protection plan as a fraction of the purchase it protects. Show the daily
cost next to the cost of the coffee it replaces. Place the recommended tier between a smaller
and a larger one.

### Similarity and unity
**Mechanism.** "People like us do things like this." Readers trust and follow those they see
as their own group.
**Works when.** The copy uses the group's language, references its shared experiences, and
shows members of the group succeeding.
**Backfires when.** The group identity is asserted from outside and reads as pandering.
**Apply.** Use the reader's vocabulary. Show proof from people in the same role. Name the
shared frustration before naming the product.

### Ownership language
**Mechanism.** People value what they already own more than what they might acquire. Copy
that lets the reader mentally own the outcome makes giving it up feel like a loss.
**Works when.** Used in the After section and around the CTA.
**Backfires when.** Presumptuous before the reader has any investment.
**Apply.** "Your first report" not "a first report". "See my plan" on the button. Present
tense in the After: "You close the laptop at 5:30."

### Choice reduction
**Mechanism.** Every option is a decision, and every decision is a chance to defer. Fewer
choices convert faster.
**Works when.** One primary path is clearly marked.
**Backfires when.** Reduction hides an option the reader needed.
**Apply.** One CTA per piece. Three pricing tiers, one marked. One question per form step.

## 3. Framing rules that cut across levers

- **Gain frames for acquisition, loss frames for retention.** A new reader responds to what
  they will get. An existing customer responds to what they would lose.
- **Odd and specific numbers read as measured.** "4,213 customers" is believed; "4,000+" is
  believed less; "thousands" is not believed. Never round a real number down to sound cleaner,
  and never invent a specific one to sound measured.
- **Second person throughout, except the button.** Body copy: "you". Button: "my".
- **The brand is the guide.** Count "we" against "you". If "we" wins, the copy is about the
  wrong protagonist.
- **State the mechanism once.** Readers need to know why it works. They do not need to know
  how it is built.

## 4. Where persuasion ends and manipulation begins

The levers above are how humans decide, and copy that ignores them is copy that fails. The
same levers used dishonestly produce a sale and then a refund, a chargeback, a one-star review,
and a reader who never trusts the brand again. The line:

- A lever applied to a **true fact** is persuasion. Loss framing of a real cost. Scarcity of a
  real limit. Proof from a real customer.
- A lever applied to an **invented fact** is manipulation. Any fabricated number, deadline,
  testimonial, or comparison.
- A lever that **hides the real question** is manipulation. A CTA that obscures a charge. A
  curiosity gap with no payoff. A small yes that was a hidden opt-in.

When in doubt, write the version a customer could read after buying and still feel well
treated.
