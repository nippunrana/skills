# Channel Playbooks

Read the section for the channel in the brief before drafting. Character limits are hard
limits; count before delivering. Platform limits change over time, so verify against the
platform's current documentation before anything is published.

---

## 1. Search ads (Google responsive search ads)

**Structure.** Up to 15 headlines and 4 descriptions. The platform combines them, so every
headline must stand alone and make sense next to any other headline.

**Limits.** Headlines: 30 characters each. Descriptions: 90 characters each. Display path
fields: 15 characters each.

**Rules.**
- The reader typed a query. The first headline should contain or closely match it. They are
  solution-aware or product-aware by definition; lead with the offer or the differentiator,
  not the problem.
- Write at least three headline types: keyword-matched, benefit-led, and proof-or-offer-led.
  The platform needs variety to combine.
- Put the CTA in a description, not a headline. Descriptions have room for the effort line.
- No headline should depend on another to make sense. "And it's free" is not a headline.
- Pin a headline to position 1 only when the brand or keyword must always appear.
- Deliver as a table with the character count beside each line.

**Example set** (each line counted):
- "Payroll Done in 10 Minutes" (26)
- "For Teams of 2 to 50" (20)
- "No Setup Fee. Cancel Any Time" (29)
- Description: "Run your first payroll today. Connects to your bank in two steps. Free for 30 days." (84)

## 2. Social ads (Meta, Instagram, LinkedIn, X)

**Structure.** Primary text (above the creative), headline (below the creative), optional
description, CTA button from a fixed list.

**Limits that matter.** The primary text truncates on mobile after roughly 125 characters on
Meta and roughly 150 on LinkedIn, behind a "see more" link. The hook lives in that window.
Headline: about 40 characters on Meta before truncation, about 70 on LinkedIn.

**Rules.**
- The reader did not ask for this. They are unaware or problem-aware. The first line must
  create self-recognition or open a curiosity gap; never lead with the product name.
- The first line is the whole ad. If it does not stop the scroll, nothing after it is read.
- Short lines. One idea per line. Line breaks are free and they slow the scroll.
- The headline under the creative restates the offer or the big idea, not the hook.
- The CTA button is chosen from the platform's list; write the copy so the button label
  ("Learn more", "Sign up", "Shop now") reads as the natural next step.
- Long primary text can work for high-consideration offers once the hook has earned it.
  Use PAS or a story lead. Keep paragraphs to two lines.
- LinkedIn readers are in a professional frame. Self-recognition by role and situation
  outperforms emotional hooks. X readers reward directness and brevity.

**Example hook lines** (within the truncation window):
- "Every Monday you rebuild the same report from last week's exports."
- "Most meal plans fail on Thursday. Here is why."
- "For the person who owns the on-call rota and never gets thanked for it."

## 3. Display and banner ads

Five to seven words in the headline. One line of support if there is room. One CTA. The
reader gives a banner less than a second; specificity and self-recognition are the only levers
that work. No paragraphs, no lists, no more than one number.

---

## 4. Landing page sections

For each section, the job, the length, and the rules. Full sequence in
`copy-frameworks.md`.

**Hero**
- Eyebrow: three to six words. Category or who it is for.
- Headline: ten words or fewer. The big idea. Passes the 4U test.
- Subheadline: one to two sentences. The mechanism and the first objection answered.
- CTA button: first person, light verb. Effort line beneath.
- Proof snippet: one number or one named customer. Only if real.

**Benefit blocks**
- Three at most. Each has a benefit headline (not a feature name) of six words or fewer, then
  one or two sentences. The sentence passes the so-that test.

**How it works**
- Three numbered steps. One line each. The first step must sound like something the reader
  could do right now. "Connect your calendar" beats "Configure your integration settings".

**Testimonials**
- Structure each as: outcome first, then who said it and their role. Never lead with the
  name. Cut any testimonial that does not contain a specific result or a specific objection
  overcome. Never write a testimonial; only place real ones the user supplies.

**Pricing copy**
- Tier names describe who the tier is for, not how big it is. A one-line "best for" under each
  tier name. The recommended tier is marked and the reason is stated. Savings stated as an
  amount, not a percentage, and the arithmetic done for the reader.

**FAQ**
- Each question is an objection phrased the way the reader phrases it: "What happens when my
  trial ends?" not "Trial terms". Answers are two to three sentences and end with the
  reassuring fact, not a link.

**Final CTA**
- A new headline angle. If the hero was outcome-led, this one is pain-led or proof-led. Same
  button, same action.

## 5. Email

**Subject line.** Under about 50 characters so it survives a phone screen. One job: the open.
Patterns in `copy-frameworks.md`.

**Preheader.** 40 to 90 characters. Extends the subject line with new information. Never
repeats it, never "View this email in your browser".

**Body.**
- One idea. One CTA. If there are two things to say, send two emails.
- Open with the reader's situation or the payoff of the subject line, never with "I hope this
  finds you well" or an introduction of the sender.
- Short paragraphs, one to three lines. Plenty of white space.
- The CTA is a link or button in the first screen and repeated once near the end.
- A postscript line is the second most read line after the subject. Use it for the one
  objection or the one deadline, if real.

**Cold outreach.** Under 100 words. Self-recognition in line one (their situation, not your
company). One specific reason you are writing to them. One small ask. No attachments, no
multiple links.

---

## 6. Product descriptions

- Lead with the benefit and the situation of use, not the product name. "Coffee that stays
  hot through a two-hour meeting" before "16 oz insulated tumbler".
- Sensory specifics. Weight, texture, sound, what it feels like in the hand.
- Specifications as so-that sentences. "Double-wall steel, so that the outside never gets hot."
- Answer the fit objection: size, compatibility, who it is not for.
- Two to four short paragraphs, or one paragraph plus a bulleted spec list. Bullets are for
  specs; benefits go in prose.

---

## 7. Microcopy

Microcopy is copy at the point of friction. Its job is reassurance.

- **Buttons.** First person, specific object. "Save my changes" over "Submit".
- **Form labels.** Say what and why in one line where the why is not obvious: "Phone number
  (only used for delivery updates)".
- **Error messages.** State what happened and what to do next. Never blame the reader. "That
  card was declined. Try another card or contact your bank."
- **Empty states.** Say what will appear here and how to make the first one. Include the
  first action as a button.
- **Confirmation.** State what happens next and when. "Your order ships tomorrow. We will
  email tracking by 6pm."
- **Tooltips.** One sentence. If it needs two, the interface needs a label instead.

---

## 8. Taglines and slogans

Seven words or fewer. Concrete over abstract. Ownable: it should not be true of a competitor.
Either a promise ("Ships today") or a stance ("Built for the night shift"). Never a pun that
needs explaining. Write thirty, keep two, test them by saying them aloud in the sentence
"[Brand]: [tagline]".
