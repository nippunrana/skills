# Copy Frameworks, Headline Formulas, and CTA Patterns

Read this before drafting anything longer than a single line. Pick one framework per piece.
Mixing frameworks produces copy with no spine.

---

## 1. Structural frameworks

### PAS: Pain, Agitate, Solve
Best for problem-aware readers. The most reliable framework for short ads and landing pages.

1. **Pain.** Name the problem in the reader's words. Specific, not general. "Your invoices go
   out late" not "cash flow challenges".
2. **Agitate.** Make the cost of the problem vivid. What it costs per week. What it will cost in
   a year. What it does to the reader's standing with the people they answer to. Stay honest;
   agitation is amplification of a real cost, not invention of a fake one.
3. **Solve.** Present the product as the exit. State the mechanism in one line, then the
   outcome, then the action.

Failure mode: spending too long in Pain and too little in Solve. The reader already knows the
pain. Two lines of pain, one of agitation, then move.

### AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action
Best for cold readers and longer pieces where the reader must be walked in.

1. **Attention.** One line that stops the scroll. Specificity or self-recognition.
2. **Interest.** Why this matters to them right now. The "so what".
3. **Desire.** The Dream Outcome made concrete. What Tuesday looks like after.
4. **Action.** One CTA, first person, with the effort quantified.

Failure mode: Attention that has nothing to do with the offer. A hook that does not lead to the
product is a hook the reader resents.

### Before-After Bridge
Best for transformation products: fitness, finance, education, coaching, anything where the
buyer is buying a future self.

1. **Before.** The current state, in enough detail that the reader recognizes their week.
2. **After.** The destination. Vivid, specific, sensory. This is where most of the copy's
   energy goes.
3. **Bridge.** The product as the mechanism between the two. Short. The After sells; the
   Bridge just explains how.

Failure mode: an After that is generic. "A better life" is not an After. "Closing the laptop at
5:30 and not opening it again" is.

### 4Ps: Promise, Picture, Proof, Push
Best for solution-aware readers who need to be convinced this version works.

1. **Promise.** The outcome, in one line.
2. **Picture.** What having the outcome is like. Second person, present tense.
3. **Proof.** Evidence the promise holds. Real numbers, named results, or the mechanism
   explained. If there is no proof, the mechanism does the work.
4. **Push.** The CTA and, if real, the reason to act now.

### FAB: Feature, Advantage, Benefit
Not a page framework. A sentence-level tool for turning specifications into copy. Use it in
feature sections and product descriptions.

- **Feature:** what it is. "Titanium frame."
- **Advantage:** what that does. "Weighs 40% less than steel."
- **Benefit:** what that means for the reader. "Carry it up four flights without noticing."

Deliver the Benefit. Mention the Feature. Cut the Advantage unless the reader is technical.

### Story lead
Best for unaware readers and brand-level pieces. The reader is the hero. The brand is the
guide who hands them the tool.

1. **A character with a want.** The reader, in a recognizable situation.
2. **A problem in the way.** External (the task), internal (how it makes them feel), and
   philosophical (why it is unfair that they have to deal with it).
3. **A guide appears.** The brand, shown through empathy and authority, never through
   self-praise.
4. **A plan.** Three steps, no more.
5. **A call to action.** Direct.
6. **What is at stake.** Success if they act. The cost if they do not.

Failure mode: the brand becomes the hero. If the copy says "we" more than "you", rewrite it.

## 2. Headline formulas

Every headline must pass the **4U test**: Useful (offers something the reader wants), Urgent
(a reason to read now), Unique (says something competitors do not), Ultra-specific (a number,
a detail, a named situation). Three of four is acceptable. Two of four is a rewrite.

**End result + time period + emotional payoff**
"Get a lean body in 45 minutes a day and feel good in your clothes."
Works when the time period is real and short.

**How to [outcome] without [pain]**
"How to ship every Friday without a single late-night deploy."
The "without" clause answers the effort objection inside the headline.

**Stop [pain]**
"Stop rebuilding the same report every Monday."
Direct, problem-aware. Works when the pain is universal for the persona.

**For [reader] who [situation]**
"For founders who still do their own bookkeeping."
Self-recognition. The reader qualifies themself in.

**[Number] [things] that [outcome]**
"Seven questions that tell you whether a candidate will quit in six months."
Numbers signal specificity and a finite commitment to read.

**Finally, [outcome the reader gave up on]**
"Finally, a calendar your whole team actually looks at."
Works for solution-aware readers who have tried and failed.

**[X], not [Y]**
"Built for the night shift, not adapted for it."
Contrast headline. Differentiates on mechanism or intent.

**Question the reader answers yes to**
"Still exporting to a spreadsheet to see last month's numbers?"
Only if the honest answer is yes for the persona. A "no" ends the read.

**Even if [objection]**
"Learn to draw, even if you cannot draw a straight line."
Answers the fit objection inside the headline.

### Headline rules
- Ten words or fewer for a hero. Search ad headlines have a hard character limit; see
  `channel-playbooks.md`.
- Concrete nouns and active verbs. No adjectives that could be replaced by a number.
- The headline states the big idea. The subheadline explains how. Never split one idea across
  both.
- Write twenty, keep three. The first five are always the obvious ones.

## 3. Subheadline patterns

The subheadline's job is to reduce perceived effort and answer the first objection.

- **Without [pain]:** "Without changing your existing tools."
- **In [time]:** "Set up in four minutes, first report in five."
- **Even if [objection]:** "Even if your data lives in six places."
- **[How it works] so that [benefit]:** "It reads your calendar so that you never double-book."
- **For [who]:** "For teams of two to twenty who outgrew the spreadsheet."

One or two sentences. If the headline is outcome-led, the subheadline is mechanism-led, and
the reverse.

## 4. CTA formulas

A CTA is three parts: the button, the effort line beneath it, and the risk-reversal line.

**Button**
First person, light verb, the specific object.
- "Start my free trial"
- "Show me my plan"
- "Get the checklist"
- "See pricing for my team"

Verbs to prefer: Start, Get, See, Show, Try, Send, Book. Verbs to avoid on first touch:
Subscribe, Submit, Sign up, Register, Buy (unless the reader is most-aware).

**Effort line** (directly under the button)
Quantify the effort or remove the hidden question.
- "No credit card. Takes 2 minutes."
- "Works with the tools you already use."
- "Cancel any time before day 14 with one click."

**Risk reversal** (near the button, or near payment)
Only what is true.
- "30-day money-back guarantee, no questions asked."
- "We email you three days before your trial ends."

**CTA sequencing for multi-step pieces**
Small yes before big yes. A page can ask "See how it works" before it asks "Start my trial".
The second ask is easier once the first has been answered. Never place two asks of equal
weight side by side.

## 5. Email subject line patterns

The subject line has one job: get the open. The preheader has one job: extend the subject
line, not repeat it.

- **Curiosity with payoff inside:** "The Thursday problem with meal plans"
- **Specific benefit:** "Your invoices out by 9am, every Monday"
- **Self-recognition:** "For the person who owns the on-call rota"
- **Direct question:** "Still doing payroll by hand?"
- **Number lead:** "3 lines that doubled our reply rate"

Rules: under about 50 characters so it survives a phone screen. No ALL CAPS, no more than one
punctuation mark, no words that read like spam ("free", "act now", "guaranteed") in the subject
line itself. Lowercase or sentence case reads as personal.

## 6. Landing page copy sequence

When the deliverable is full-page copy, deliver these sections in order and give each a
one-line job. Cut any section whose job cannot be stated.

1. **Hero:** eyebrow (category or who it is for), headline (big idea), subheadline (how, and
   the first objection), CTA with effort line, one proof snippet.
2. **Proof bar:** named customers or a single specific result. Only if real.
3. **Problem:** the pain in the reader's words, agitated once. Skip for most-aware readers.
4. **Benefits:** three at most. Benefit headlines, not feature names. One sentence each.
5. **How it works:** three numbered steps. Each step one line. The first step must sound
   trivially easy.
6. **Proof:** testimonials or results. Outcome first, then who said it. Only real ones.
7. **Objections:** an FAQ where each question is an objection phrased the way the reader
   phrases it.
8. **Offer and CTA:** the terms, the guarantee, the button. A new headline angle, not a
   repeat of the hero.

Repeat the CTA after every two or three sections. Every repeat uses the same action and a
different lead-in line.
