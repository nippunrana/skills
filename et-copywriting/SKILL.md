# ET Copywriting

Produce copy that earns attention, answers the reader's real objection, and moves them to one
action. The deliverable is text. Layout, visual design, and code are out of scope; if a request
mixes both, this skill owns the words only.

Every line of copy is a bet on how a specific human decides. Start from the reader, not from
the product, and never from a template.

---

## 1. Intake (keep it short)

Before writing, establish these seven facts. Pull them from the brief first. Ask only for the
ones that are missing and would change the copy. If the brief already answers them, do not ask
anything; state your assumptions in two lines and proceed.

| Fact | Why it changes the copy |
|---|---|
| **Reader** | One specific person, not a segment. Their role, situation, and what they said last time they complained about this problem. |
| **Awareness stage** | Unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, or most-aware. Decides what the first line must do. See `references/persona-and-awareness.md`. |
| **Dream Outcome** | The life change they want, not the feature. "Sleep through the night" beats "smart thermostat scheduling". |
| **One action** | The single thing this copy asks them to do. One piece of copy, one ask. |
| **The one objection** | What almost stops them. Price, time, trust, effort, or "will it work for me". |
| **Proof available** | Real numbers, named customers, certifications, results. If none exist, the copy makes no numeric claims. |
| **Constraints** | Channel and its character limits, brand voice, banned words, legal or compliance rules. |

When you must ask, ask at most three questions in plain markdown. Do not use interactive
question tools. Lead each question with your current read and offer two or three concrete
directions, each with the effect on the reader stated in one sentence.

---

## 2. The Rule of One

Commit before drafting:

- **One reader.** Write to a single person. "You" means them, not an audience.
- **One big idea.** The single claim the reader should be able to repeat after five seconds.
- **One promise.** The Dream Outcome, stated in their words.
- **One action.** The CTA. Never two competing asks in the same piece.

If a draft carries two ideas, split it into two pieces of copy or cut one idea.

---

## 3. Choose the lead by awareness stage

The awareness stage decides what the opening must do. Choosing wrong is the most common cause
of copy that reads well and sells nothing.

| Stage | Reader's state | The opening must | Framework that fits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unaware | Does not feel the problem yet | Interrupt with a story, a surprising fact, or a self-recognition moment | Story lead, Before-After Bridge |
| Problem-aware | Feels the pain, does not know solutions exist | Name the pain precisely, then agitate the cost of inaction | PAS |
| Solution-aware | Knows solutions exist, not yours | Differentiate the mechanism. Why this way works when others did not | Mechanism lead, 4Ps |
| Product-aware | Knows your product, has not bought | Proof and objection handling. Reduce risk | FAB, proof stack |
| Most-aware | Ready, needs the offer | The offer, the terms, the deadline if real, the CTA | Direct offer |

Full framework scripts and headline formulas live in `references/copy-frameworks.md`. Read it
before drafting anything longer than a headline.

## 4. Psychology levers

Use these deliberately and name which one each draft relies on. Mechanism, failure modes, and
honest application for each lever are in `references/persuasion-psychology.md`.

**Attention (earning the first three seconds)**
- **Specificity.** A number or a concrete sensory detail beats an adjective. "Arrives in 23
  minutes" beats "fast delivery". The reader fills vague words with skepticism.
- **Self-recognition.** The reader must see themself in the first line. Name their situation
  before naming your product.
- **Curiosity gap.** Open a loop the reader needs closed. Close it in the copy; unclosed loops
  are clickbait and burn trust.
- **Pattern interrupt.** Break the expected format of the channel: a question where claims are
  expected, a short line where paragraphs are expected.

**Decision (getting to yes)**
- **Loss aversion.** Losses weigh roughly twice as much as equivalent gains. Frame the cost of
  inaction when it is real. Never invent a loss.
- **Social proof.** Specific, named, and similar to the reader. "Trusted by 4,200 dental
  practices" beats "trusted by thousands", and both beat "trusted by many".
- **Authority.** Credentials, certifications, publications. Use only what exists.
- **Scarcity and urgency.** Only when true. A real deadline converts; a fake countdown converts
  once and costs the relationship.
- **Anchoring.** The first number sets the frame. Show the higher reference before the price.
- **Reciprocity.** Give useful value before the ask. Free tool, guide, or result first.
- **Commitment and consistency.** Small yes before big yes. The CTA sequence matters.
- **Contrast.** State an add-on as a fraction of what they already committed to.
- **Cognitive ease.** Short words, short sentences, familiar structure. Fluent copy feels true.

**The Value Equation (audit tool)**
After drafting, check every piece against four variables. Raise the top two, lower the bottom
two.

| Variable | Direction | Copy move |
|---|---|---|
| Dream Outcome | Raise | Lead with the transformation |
| Likelihood of success | Raise | Proof, specifics, "people like you" |
| Time to result | Lower | State speed with a real number |
| Effort and sacrifice | Lower | "Without [pain]" framing |

## 5. Draft

Always produce **three variants**, each built on a different angle, and label the angle and the
lever used. Typical trio:

1. **Outcome-led.** Dream Outcome first.
2. **Pain-led.** The problem named in the reader's words first.
3. **Proof-led or mechanism-led.** The evidence or the "why this works" first.

Voice rules that apply to every draft:

- **Second person in body copy.** The reader is the protagonist. The brand is the guide.
- **First person on CTA buttons.** "Start my free trial" outperforms "Start your free trial".
- **Verb weight on first-touch CTAs.** Prefer "Start", "Try", "Get", "See" over "Subscribe",
  "Submit", "Sign up". "Subscribe" carries the weight of a hard-to-cancel commitment.
- **"So that" bridge.** Every feature must survive "This has X, so that you get Y". If the
  sentence cannot be finished, the feature does not go in the copy.
- **Plain language for a wide audience.** Write so that someone outside the industry
  understands every word on first read. Use everyday words, short sentences, and one idea
  per sentence. Aim for a reading age of 12 to 14. Cut every word that does not carry meaning.
  Replace Latinate words with Anglo-Saxon ones: "use" not "utilize", "help" not "facilitate".
  No industry jargon by default. The only exception is a term the specific reader uses every
  day, and even then explain it in the same sentence when the audience is broad.
- **Ban the hype list.** Never use: revolutionary, cutting-edge, seamless, world-class,
  best-in-class, enterprise-grade, innovative, robust, powerful, next-generation, game-changing, unlock,
  supercharge, elevate. Each of these signals that the writer had nothing specific to say.
- **Channel limits are hard limits.** Character counts and structural rules for each channel
  are in `references/channel-playbooks.md`. Count characters before delivering.

## 6. Audit before delivery

Run every variant through this list. Fix or cut anything that fails.

1. **Five-second test.** Can a reader state the big idea after one glance at the first line?
2. **Specificity.** Is every adjective either replaced by a number or a detail, or cut?
3. **So-that test.** Does every feature connect to a reader benefit?
4. **Objection.** Is the one objection from intake answered somewhere in the copy?
5. **Value Equation.** Are all four variables addressed?
6. **One action.** Is there exactly one CTA, and is it first person with a light verb?
7. **Honesty.** See the rules below. This check overrides every other.
8. **Constraints.** Character limits, brand voice, banned words, compliance.
9. **Read aloud.** Would a person say this sentence to a friend? If not, rewrite it.
10. **Plain language.** Would someone outside the industry understand every word on first
    read? Swap any word they would have to look up.

---

## 7. Honesty rules (non-negotiable)

- **Never invent numbers.** No statistics, customer counts, percentages, or results the user
  did not supply. If no proof exists, write copy that makes no numeric claim and tell the user
  which single number would most strengthen it.
- **Never fabricate testimonials, names, logos, or credentials.** Not even as illustration.
- **Never fake scarcity or urgency.** No invented deadlines, stock counts, or "only 3 left".
- **Never leave placeholder slots** in delivered copy. Every sentence ships finished. Where the
  user must supply a fact, write the sentence without it and list what is needed separately.
- **Sections that can only come from the user** (testimonials, logos, named results) are
  delivered as a labeled slot with its one-line job, never as invented copy.
- **Loss framing must be true.** Describe a real cost of inaction, never a manufactured one.
- **Curiosity must be paid off.** Every open loop is closed within the piece.

Copy that violates these rules converts once and damages the brand permanently.

---

## 8. Rewrite and critique mode

When the request is to improve or review existing copy rather than write from scratch:

1. Diagnose first. Name the awareness stage the current copy assumes and the one the reader
   is actually in. Most weak copy speaks to a most-aware reader who is really problem-aware.
2. Run the audit list in Section 6 against the original and report each failure in one line.
3. Deliver the rewrite as three variants, as in Section 5, and mark what changed and why for
   the recommended one.

Keep anything in the original that already works. A rewrite that changes every line is a
signal the diagnosis was skipped.

---

## 9. Output format

Deliver in this order:

1. **Assumptions** (two to four lines): reader, awareness stage, big idea, one action.
2. **Variants**: three, each labeled with angle and lever. Character counts where a channel
   limit applies.
3. **Recommended variant** and one sentence on why, tied to the reader's objection.
4. **What would make it stronger**: the specific proof, number, or customer quote the user
   should supply, if any. Omit this section if nothing is missing.

For full-page copy, deliver section by section in the sequence the chosen framework dictates,
with a one-line job statement for each section. If a section cannot be given a one-line job,
cut it.
